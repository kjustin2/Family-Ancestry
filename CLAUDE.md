# Family-history writing and visual standard

- Keep pages concise. Put a simple, purposeful visualization near any long lineage, timeline, geographic account, or series of money figures. A reader should understand its point within seconds.
- For a family line, **pair the visual and a short human story in the same reading view**. Where sources permit, put a dated work clue, an asset or cost observation, and an education clue beside that generation; label a missing measure instead of drawing a misleading line. Link each scene to its artifact or original record and the fuller branch page. Maintain a combined view across Justin's and Ashley's branches so readers can choose a line before opening detailed files.
- Use diagrams to show relationships and confidence, maps to show where people lived, timelines to show sequence, and charts to show comparable values over time. Give each visual a descriptive title, readable labels, alt text, and a short source/caveat caption. Make them attractive without decoration that obscures evidence.
- For family trees, follow each line back to its earliest supported or clearly labeled proposed generation. Use several simple linked trees when one diagram would be crowded; do not stop at the nearest one or two generations when older research exists. Draw uncertain parent or identity links differently, and link the diagram to its source notes.
- Do not imply a relationship, visit, school experience, income, inheritance, or inflation-adjusted trend that the cited records do not establish. Label family accounts, proposed connections, and documented facts directly on the visual.
- Disambiguate repeated names with stable names and years everywhere. In the Kramer line use **Ferdinand (c. 1882)**, **Ferdinand Louis (1913)**, and **Fred / Ferdinand Francis (1939)**; the birth years are chart/obituary identifiers, not official generational suffixes.
- Keep editable SVG sources in `maps/`, check that they render legibly, and link the underlying source records in the accompanying Markdown.
- Use small, clickable **period images** when they make a school, job, street or migration setting easier to imagine. Caption them as a family portrait, family document, place view or period advertisement; give date, source and reuse credit, and state clearly whether a relative is actually identified. A contextual image should add an observable detail, not merely decorate a long page.
- Save and surface the actual family artifact or original record image when permitted, especially Bibles, certificates, portraits, letters and contemporary biographies. Pair each with one concise observation about work, character, choices or a remembered moment, explicitly attributed to the document or storyteller. Link the full scan and explain uncertain identity or retrospective claims.
